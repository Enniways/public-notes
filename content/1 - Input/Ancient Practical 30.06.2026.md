# Virtue according to Epicuru

## Courage (minimse fear!)

Just as you should minimise wants, you should also minimise any fear.
What reason would an Epicurean have to risk bodily harm or pain?

- There does not seem much to defend - Letter to Menoeceus 130-1.
- But we do need future and continued security and this seeems to require continued support of our property, friend, family, and country. Thus, courageous behavior might be part of an Epicurean life, even if the things it would defend are of only instrumental value.
  → **Cowardace is probably way more painful than experiencing bodily harm.**

## Justice

(Justice as a pledge among the members of society not to harm and so not to be harmed by, one another. (Principal Doctrines, 31-33)

- But why act on this in cases our interests conflict with the interests of others if, as Epicurus claims, virtue is not valuable in itself?
  - "It is impossible for one who violates any of the things agreed for the sake of not harming or being harbmed by one another t obe confident that he will escape detention, even if from the present time forward he has tens of thousands of escapes. For it is unclear up to death whether he will in fact escape detention" (Principal Doctrines, 35)
  - So, **injustice would do away with peace of mind and so with katastematic pleasures.**

# Zeno of Citium

- born around _333 BCE_ in cyprus
- He comes to [[Athens]] at around 311BCE (very likely he met Socrates)
- He begins "pacing up and down" at Stoa Poikile at around 300 BCE.
- First contact with philosophy: Socrates in Xenophon and, subsequently a pupil of Crates, a cynic philosopher.
  - From them - knowledge and goodness are intimately connected.
- Second stage: The Megarians (Stilpo and Diodorus Cronus) and the Academy (Polemo)
  - From the Megarians: interest in logic and language & in monism
  - From the academy: psychology and moral they; theology (Timaeus)
  - Influence of Heraclitus: acknowledged by Zeno
- He dies 261 BCE in Athens.
- Works: Life According to Nature, Emotions, Taht which is appropriate, Homeric Problems, Universals, Greek Culture, and so on

# Stoic Living in Agreement wih Oneself

alias Origin of Moral Duty
Fully virtuous living (or happy living) involves agreement with oneself.

Homologoumenos: "having reasoned thoughts that are the same or in common". This then includes following Zeus' wishes as to how one should live - and so doing the god-given duties

The goal is living thinking in some way the same thoughts that the world itself does

**The highest good, as the Stoics conceive of it, can be characterised as a life devoted to moral duty** → The first conception of moral duty
This characterization of the life of virtue which they, as well as Plato and Aristotle regard as the highest good, is a novelty.

**Moral Duty ≠ Religious Duty** at first. Gods can demand bad things.

# Stoic Living in Accordance with Nature

alias Stoic conception of Science, Stoic ethical naturalism

There is no book of how things you should do things. **Science is just discovering the divine Plan**

- Not the same as [[Stoic Living in Agreement with Oneself]]
- "Nature" here is not the world-mind itself. It means living in accordance with the natural outcomes, caused by God's thought (which is great in the end)
- These we can observe occuring over time and we can, on the basis of our observation, infer to what God or nature thinks of as the proper behavior for all living thins (including ourselves).
- This is the bsasis of Stoic "ethical naturalism".

# Stoic Eudaimonism

- The end (telos) or the highest good is "being happy" (eudaimonia)
- The end is iteslf an action - but a naction not done for the sake of anything else.
- It is, as Aristotle said, "living in accordance with virtues"

## Two further characterizations:

- The activity of the virtues is the same as living "in agreement"(homologoumenos)
- and as livin "in accordance with nature"
